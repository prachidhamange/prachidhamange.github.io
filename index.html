Microsoft · Outlook Web · Security and rollout

Moving Outlook Web's service identity off stored credentials

I owned the migration of Outlook Web's backend service identity to OS-managed virtual accounts, rolled out ring by ring across a production fleet of about 50,000 high-availability clusters.

Context

Outlook Web's backend authenticated with a legacy credential model that had built up as security debt across a live fleet. A virtual account is managed by the operating system, so there is no service password to store, rotate or leak. This work was one workstream of a larger migration of the service to .NET Core.

What made it hard

The service could not be paused while it moved. Each environment surfaced auth, permissions and secrets-management failures that only appeared once the new identity was in place. Fixing some of them needed agreement from auth and security teams in other orgs.

What I did

  • Owned the architecture and the alignment with auth and security teams across orgs.
  • Chose an incremental migration over a big-bang cutover, so a problem would stay inside one ring.
  • Designed the rollout across five environments: three pre-production stages, then production rings, with each promotion gated on telemetry and rollback ready at every ring.
  • Root-caused the failures each ring exposed before promoting the next.
  • Built dashboards tracking the health of migrated services ring by ring, using AI tooling and agent skills to build them far faster than by hand.
  • Wrote the telemetry and runbooks that let any on-call engineer operate the new identity model.

Result

Outlook Web's service identity moved to virtual accounts across the fleet one ring at a time, with each promotion gated on what the telemetry showed.

Microsoft · Outlook · Compliance

Content reports under the EU Digital Services Act

I designed and built the backend behind Outlook's Report a Concern flow, which handles content reports required under the EU Digital Services Act.

Context

The Digital Services Act puts legal obligations on how platforms act on reported content. In Outlook, when a user reports a mail, that mail has to be deleted from every recipient's mailbox, correctly, auditably and within the regulatory deadline.

What made it hard

Volume was modest, hundreds of reports a week. The difficulty sat inside each report: one mail can have hundreds of recipients, every one of those deletes has to happen, and none of them can be silently dropped.

What I did

  • Worked with legal, compliance and product to turn the legal requirements into technical contracts.
  • Modelled the flow as event-driven: reporting a mail raises an event, and that event asynchronously fans the delete out to every recipient mailbox.
  • Built parallel gRPC and REST execution paths, and authored and evolved the protobuf contracts between services.
  • Made the per-mailbox handlers idempotent, so a failed operation can be retried safely.
  • Isolated failures per mailbox, so one mailbox failing does not block or undo the others.

Result

More than 99.9% reliability against regulatory deadlines, with no report silently dropped.

  • event-driven fan-out
  • idempotency
  • partial failure
  • gRPC
  • protobuf contracts
